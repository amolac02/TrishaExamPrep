Think like a scientist: how to crack any question

  • Read the command word
  • Build a chain of reasons
  • Solve calculations step by step

Good scientists don't just know facts. They can explain why something happens and work out answers they have never seen before. These three tools are used in every worked example in this guide.

Tool 1: Spot the command word

The first word of a question tells you what kind of answer the examiner wants.

Command wordWhat it wantsExample
State / NameA short fact. No reason needed."Name the layer at the centre of the Earth." → Inner core.
DescribeSay what happens, step by step.Describe how the particles move in a sound wave.
ExplainSay why or how. Use "because", "so", "this means".Explain why sound can't travel in space.
SuggestUse your science on an unfamiliar situation; there may be several good answers.Suggest why the theatre has soft seats.
CalculateShow your working and give units.Calculate how deep the water is.
PredictSay what will happen and give a scientific reason.Predict what happens as air is pumped out of the jar.
EvaluateWeigh up good points and bad points, then make a judgement.Evaluate the polystyrene model of moving plates.

Tool 2: The reasoning chain (Claim → Evidence → Reasoning)

For "explain" questions, build a chain where every link connects to the next. Missing links are the most common way to lose marks.

Claim

Your answer in one sentence.
"We can't hear sound in space."

Evidence / fact

The science fact it depends on.
"Space is a vacuum: it has no particles."

Reasoning

The link that joins them.
"Sound is passed on by particles vibrating, so with no particles nothing can carry the vibration."

Tool 3: Calculations with G-U-E-S-S

G · Given

Write down the numbers you know, with units.

U · Unknown

What are you trying to find?

E · Equation

Pick the formula, e.g. distance = speed × time. Rearrange if needed.

S · Substitute

Put the numbers in.

S · Solve and sense-check

Work it out, add units, and ask: "Is this answer sensible?"

The speed triangle

One equation, three forms. Cover the one you want to find:

distance = speed × time
speed = distance ÷ time
time = distance ÷ speed

Echo questions: the sound goes there and back. Always ask yourself, "Is this distance one way, or the round trip?"

Station 6.1

Sound waves: the world is buzzing

  • Sound comes from vibrations
  • How particles move in a sound wave
  • Why space is silent

Every sound starts with a wobble

Put your fingers on your throat and hum. Feel that tingle? Your vocal cords are vibrating, which means moving backwards and forwards very quickly. Anything that makes a sound is vibrating: a drum skin, a guitar string, a mosquito's wings, the paper cone inside a loudspeaker.

Sprinkle rice on a drum and hit it, and the grains jump. That is the vibration made visible.

Loudness

A bigger vibration gives a louder sound. Hit a drum harder or pluck a string with more force and the sound gets louder.

Loud: thunder, a jackhammer. Quiet: a whisper, rustling leaves.

Pitch

Pitch is how high or low a sound is. A faster vibration gives a higher pitch.

High: a whistle, a baby crying. Low: thunder, a bass drum.

What is a sound wave?

Sound travels from the vibrating object to your ear as a sound wave. The vibrating object pushes on the particles of air next to it. Those particles bump into their neighbours, those bump into the next ones, and so on, like a chain of people passing on a shove.

Key point: the particles don't travel all the way to your ear. Each one just jiggles backwards and forwards around its own spot. What travels is the energy, passed from particle to particle. The particles vibrate in the same direction that the wave is moving.

In air, sound travels at about 343 m/s, the speed of sound. That's roughly the length of three football pitches every second!

Sound needs a medium

The stuff a sound wave travels through is called the medium. A sound wave needs particles to vibrate, and solids, liquids and gases all have particles, so sound travels through all three.

Gas

Your friend's voice reaching you across a room.

Liquid

Whales and dolphins call to each other underwater.

Solid

Press your ear to a table while someone taps the far end. It sounds louder through the wood!

Which medium is fastest?

Sound doesn't travel at the same speed everywhere. The closer together the particles are, the faster they can pass the vibration on, like a line of dominoes standing close together compared with far apart.

Silence in a vacuum

To hear a sound you need two things: a vibration and a medium with particles to carry it. A vacuum is completely empty space with no particles, so there is nothing to pass the vibration along. Sound cannot travel through a vacuum.

The Sun is noisy

Space is (almost) a vacuum. Scientists think that if sound could cross space, we'd hear the Sun giving off a high-pitched hum with occasional loud, low roars. Explosions in space films are fake. In real space they'd be totally silent.

Sound carries energy

Sound is a way of transferring energy. That energy can do real things:

  • Very loud sounds make the particles in air vibrate so strongly that they can damage your ears. That's why people working near jet engines wear ear defenders.
  • A powerful sound at just the right pitch can make a wine glass vibrate so much that it shatters.
  • Some vibrations are too small for our ears to detect, like a feather landing or a fly walking on a window.

Slinky sound wave

  1. Stretch a metal slinky along the floor. Mark a few coils with small paper flags; these are your "particles".
  2. Hold one end still. At the other end, push and pull your hand quickly along the length of the slinky.
  3. Watch the squashed sections race along while each flag only wiggles back and forth.

This is a model: it shows something we can't see (air particles) using something we can.

String telephone

  1. Poke a small hole in the bottom of two paper cups (ask an adult to help with the sharp object). Thread a long string through and knot it inside each cup.
  2. Pull the string tight. One person whispers into a cup; the other listens.
  3. Now let the string go slack, or pinch it in the middle. What happens?

A tight string passes the vibrations along well. A slack or pinched string stops them. To make it a fair test, try to speak at the same loudness each time (a phone playing a recorded sound works even better).

Understand it deeper

Picture this: a Newton's cradle, the desk toy with a row of hanging metal balls. Lift the first ball and let it hit the row. The middle balls barely move, yet the last ball flies out. The energy has passed through the balls by each one nudging the next. Air particles in a sound wave behave the same way: each one nudges its neighbour and stays roughly where it was.

Pitch, more precisely: scientists measure how many vibrations happen every second. This is called the frequency and it's measured in hertz (Hz). 1 Hz = 1 vibration per second. Humans can hear from about 20 Hz (very low rumble) to about 20 000 Hz (very high whine). Dogs can hear higher than that, which is why a dog whistle sounds silent to you.

How instruments change pitch

Shorter, tighter or thinner strings vibrate faster, so they give a higher pitch. That's why a guitarist presses a string against the neck (making it shorter) to play a higher note, and why a tiny violin sounds higher than a huge double bass.

How instruments change loudness

Pluck, hit or blow harder and the vibrations get bigger (we say larger amplitude). More energy goes into the sound, so it's louder. The pitch doesn't change.

Myth-busters

The air travels from the drum to my ear.
The air particles only wobble back and forth. It is the energy that travels to your ear.
Loud sounds travel faster than quiet sounds.
Loud and quiet, high and low: all sounds travel at the same speed in the same medium. Speed depends on the medium, not the sound.
Sound travels best through air.
Sound travels faster through solids and liquids, because their particles are closer together.
Pitch and loudness mean the same thing.
Pitch is about how fast the vibration is (frequency). Loudness is about how big it is (amplitude). A sound can be high and quiet, or low and loud.

Where you meet it in real life

StethoscopeDoctors listen to your heart through a tube: the sound travels through the solid and the air inside it, without spreading out.
ThunderstormsYou see lightning before you hear thunder, because light is about a million times faster than sound.
Ear defendersSoft materials absorb the energy of loud sounds before it reaches your ears.
Noise-cancelling headphonesA microphone listens to outside noise and the speaker makes an opposite vibration that cancels it out.
Double glazingThe gap between two panes of glass stops a lot of sound passing through, which is why windows near airports often have it.

The silent bell

In the bell jar demonstration you can still see the bell vibrating after the air is removed. What does that tell you about light compared with sound? (Light doesn't need particles. It can travel through a vacuum. That's how sunlight reaches us across space!)

Worked reasoning: focus on the process

Calculate · thunderDuring a storm you count 6 seconds between seeing lightning and hearing thunder. Sound travels at 340 m/s. How far away is the storm?
  1. Understand the situationLightning and thunder happen at the same moment. Light reaches you almost instantly; sound takes time. So the 6 s gap is how long the sound took to travel. (This is one-way, not an echo!)
  2. Givenspeed = 340 m/s, time = 6 s
  3. Unknowndistance
  4. Equationdistance = speed × time
  5. Substitute and solve340 × 6 = 2040 m
  6. Sense-checkRoughly 3 seconds per kilometre, so 6 s ≈ 2 km. ✓

The storm is about 2040 m (≈ 2 km) away.

Handy rule to remember: count the seconds and divide by 3 to get kilometres.

Explain · spacewalkTwo astronauts on a spacewalk float right next to each other but have to talk by radio. Explain why.
  1. Spot the command word"Explain" means I need a why, with linked reasons.
  2. Find the key science ideaWhat does sound need to travel? A medium with particles.
  3. Apply it to this situationWhat's in space? Almost nothing: it's a vacuum.
  4. Build the chainVoice makes vibrations → vibrations must be passed on by particles → no particles in space → the sound wave cannot travel → the other astronaut hears nothing.
  5. Finish the ideaWhy does radio work? Radio waves are not sound waves; like light, they can travel through a vacuum.

Sound needs particles to pass on its vibrations. Space is a vacuum with no particles, so sound can't travel between the astronauts. Radio waves can travel through a vacuum, so they use radios.

Predict · bell jarAn electric bell is ringing inside a jar. Air is slowly pumped out. Predict what you would hear, and explain your prediction.
  1. What changes?The number of air particles inside the jar goes down gradually, not all at once.
  2. What does that do?Fewer particles means less is available to pass on the vibration, so less sound energy gets out.
  3. Make a prediction that matches the changeA gradual change gives a gradual effect: the sound gets quieter and quieter, until almost nothing can be heard.
  4. Check for trapsThe bell is still vibrating the whole time (you can see it). It's the medium that's disappearing, not the vibration.
  5. ExtendWhat if air is let back in? The sound gets louder again. That makes this a reversible test of the idea.

The ringing gets gradually quieter until it almost disappears, because there are fewer and fewer particles to carry the vibrations out of the jar.

Level up: practise 6.1

Focus A cello is playing at one end of a hall. A listener hears it at the other end. What has to move for the sound to reach the listener?
The particles in the air vibrate and pass the vibration along. The air itself does not blow across the hall from the cello to the listener.
Focus True or false? (a) Sound travels through a metal railing. (b) Sound travels through sea water. (c) Sound travels through a vacuum.
(a) True. (b) True. (c) False: a vacuum has no particles to vibrate.
Practice A submarine sends a sound straight down. Which way do the water particles vibrate?
Up and down, along the same line the sound is travelling, then back again. Each particle stays roughly where it is. (If the wave travelled sideways, the particles would wobble sideways.)
Practice You hold your hand close to a loudspeaker playing loud music. What do you feel, and why?
You feel the air pulsing or vibrating against your hand. The speaker cone is vibrating and pushing the air particles, and they push on your skin.
Challenge Model a sound wave with a slinky clamped at one end. How must you move your hand, and what will the slinky look like?
Push and pull your hand along the length of the slinky (not side to side). The slinky shows areas where the coils are squashed close together, separated by areas where they're stretched apart, and these areas travel along it.
Challenge Sound travels at about 340 m/s in air but about 2950 m/s in concrete. Use ideas about particles to explain why.
In a solid like concrete the particles are very close together and strongly held, so when one vibrates it pushes on its neighbour almost straight away. In a gas the particles are far apart, so each one has to move further before it bumps into the next one. The vibration is passed on more slowly.
Challenge Why do airport ground crew wear ear defenders?
Very loud sounds make the air particles vibrate with a lot of energy. These strong vibrations can damage the delicate parts inside the ear, which can cause permanent hearing loss. Ear defenders absorb much of that energy before it reaches the ear.
Station 6.2

Echoes: sound that bounces back

  • Sound waves can be reflected
  • Useful and unwanted echoes

Bounce!

One property (feature) of all waves is that they can be reflected. Think of throwing a ball at a wall: it comes back. A sound wave does the same, except it isn't pulled down by gravity.

A reflected sound is called an echo. Clap once in an empty room with bare walls and the clap seems to last longer, then fade away. That's the sound bouncing around the walls. This change to a sound is called an effect on the sound.

Good reflectors

Large, hard, smooth, flat surfaces: glass, tiles, flat metal, smooth concrete.

Poor reflectors (absorbers)

Soft, rough or bumpy materials: thick curtains, carpet, foam, blankets. They soak up the sound energy.

Useful echoes

Bats

Bats squeak, then listen. From the time the echo takes and the direction it comes from, they work out exactly where an insect is, even in the dark. This is echolocation. Dolphins do it too.

Boats

A sound sent down from a boat reflects off the seabed or a shoal of fish. The time for the echo tells the crew how deep it is.

Medical scans

Ultrasound echoes bounce back from inside the body, letting doctors build a picture of an unborn baby without any surgery.

Worked example: how deep is the water?

A ship sends a sound down. The echo comes back after 0.4 s. Sound travels at about 1500 m/s in water.

distance = speed × time
distance = 1500 m/s × 0.4 s = 600 m  ← down and back up
depth = 600 m ÷ 2 = 300 m

Trap to avoid: the echo travels there and back, so always halve the distance.

Unwanted echoes

Sometimes echoes are unwanted. In a recording studio, a single note would repeat and spoil the recording, so the walls are covered with bumpy foam shapes that absorb sound. Theatres use soft seats, curtains and curved, uneven surfaces so the audience hears the actors clearly, without big flat walls to cause echoes.

Find the bounce

  1. Lay two cardboard tubes on a table in a V shape, with both open ends pointing at a hard surface (like a tile or book), and a barrier between the tubes.
  2. Put a phone playing a quiet sound at the end of one tube.
  3. Listen at the end of the other tube and turn it until the sound is loudest. Mark the angles.
  4. Swap the hard surface for something soft, like foam or a jumper. What changes?

You should find the angles of the two tubes match, just like a ball bouncing. The soft surface gives a much weaker echo.

Echo detectives: reading the data

Scientists measure loudness in decibels (dB). The bigger the number, the louder the sound. Here is an imaginary investigation: the same clap was made in front of four different surfaces, and the loudness of each echo was measured.

Understand it deeper

Reflect, absorb or pass through? When a sound wave hits a surface, three things can happen to its energy. Most surfaces do a bit of each:

Reflected

Bounces back. Hard, smooth surfaces reflect most of the energy, so you hear a strong echo.

Absorbed

Soaked up. Soft, fluffy or holey materials trap air; the vibrations get used up inside them and the energy ends up as a tiny bit of heat.

Transmitted

Passes through, which is why you can hear your neighbour's music through a wall.

Why don't you hear an echo in your bedroom? Your brain can only tell two sounds apart if they arrive about 0.1 s or more apart. In a small room the reflection comes back much faster than that, so it blends with the original sound and just makes it sound fuller. To hear a separate echo, the wall needs to be roughly 17 m or more away (see the worked example below).

Myth-busters

An echo is louder than the original sound.
An echo is always quieter: some energy is absorbed by the surface, and the sound spreads out as it travels.
An echo is a new, different sound.
It's the same sound wave, reflected back. That's why it has the same pitch.
In echo sums, the distance is just speed × time.
speed × time gives the round trip. To get the distance to the object, halve it.

Where you meet it in real life

Car parking sensorsA sensor on the bumper sends out sound pulses too high for us to hear. The faster the echo returns, the closer the wall, and the faster the beeping.
Fishing boatsEcho sounders show shoals of fish on a screen, because fish reflect sound differently from the seabed.
Ultrasound scansHigh-pitched sound reflects off organs and a baby inside the body. It's safe enough to use during pregnancy.
Concert hallsDesigners balance hard and soft surfaces: a little reflection makes music sound rich; too much makes it muddy.
Mapping the sea floorShips use sonar to map underwater mountains. This is how the mid-ocean ridges in 6.3 were discovered!

The empty hall

A school hall sounds "echoey" when it's empty, but fine during assembly when it's full of students. Why? (People and their clothes are soft. They absorb sound energy, so less is reflected off the floor and walls.)

Worked reasoning: focus on the process

Calculate · shortest echoYour brain needs at least 0.1 s between two sounds to hear them separately. Sound travels at 340 m/s. How far away must a wall be for you to hear a clear echo?
  1. Picture the journeyDraw yourself, a wall, and an arrow going there and an arrow coming back. The 0.1 s is for the whole round trip.
  2. Givenspeed = 340 m/s, time (round trip) = 0.1 s
  3. Find the round-trip distancedistance = speed × time = 340 × 0.1 = 34 m
  4. Convert to one-wayThe wall is half of that: 34 ÷ 2 = 17 m
  5. Sense-checkA classroom is about 8–10 m long. That's less than 17 m, which matches the fact that we don't hear clear echoes in classrooms. ✓

The wall must be at least 17 m away.

Calculate · measuring the speed of soundA class stands 100 m from a large wall. The echo of a clap returns after 0.6 s. Use this to work out the speed of sound.
  1. Spot what's differentUsually we're given the speed. This time it's the unknown, so we rearrange: speed = distance ÷ time.
  2. Which distance?The sound goes to the wall and back, so the distance is 100 + 100 = 200 m.
  3. Substitutespeed = 200 ÷ 0.6
  4. Solve≈ 333 m/s
  5. Sense-check and evaluateThat's close to 340 m/s, but a little low. Why? Starting and stopping a stopwatch by hand adds a reaction-time error. Improvement: repeat several times and take an average, or time 10 echoes and divide by 10.

Speed ≈ 333 m/s. Close to the true value; the difference is likely due to human reaction time.

Plan · fair testYou want to find out which material is best at absorbing sound. How would you make your investigation a fair test?
  1. Identify what you change (independent variable)The material placed in front of the wall: foam, cardboard, tile, cloth…
  2. Identify what you measure (dependent variable)The loudness of the echo in dB, using a sound meter or phone app.
  3. List what must stay the same (control variables)Same sound (use a recording, not a clap), same volume, same distance from sound to surface, same angle, same size of material, same position for the meter.
  4. Plan for reliable resultsRepeat each reading 3 times and calculate the mean. Note any odd results (anomalies) and repeat them.
  5. Decide how to show the resultsA bar chart, because the materials are categories, not numbers.

Change only the material; keep everything else the same; measure echo loudness; repeat and average.

Focus Which of these would reflect sound best: a hedge, long grass, a smooth brick wall, or a sand dune?
The smooth brick wall, because it is hard, flat and smooth. The others are soft, uneven or full of gaps, so they absorb or scatter the sound.
Focus You stamp your foot in a long concrete underpass and hear the stamp again a moment later. Why?
The sound wave travels to the hard walls and ceiling, reflects, and comes back to your ears a short time later. That's an echo.

In the tunnel. It is surrounded by hard, flat surfaces close by that reflect the sound back. In a field the sound spreads out, and there are no nearby hard surfaces to bounce it back.

Challenge Leo stands 170 m from a tall cliff and bangs a drum. Sound travels at 340 m/s in air. How long until he hears the echo?
total distance = 170 m there + 170 m back = 340 m

Challenge Submarine crews can't see in deep, dark water. How can echoes help them find a large rock ahead?
The submarine sends out a sound pulse. When the pulse hits the rock it reflects back as an echo. The time the echo takes tells them how far away the rock is (longer time = further away), and the direction the echo comes from tells them where it is. This is called sonar.
Station 6.3

Inside the Earth: a planet in layers

  • A model of Earth's structure
  • How the continents have moved

Scientists have worked out that the Earth is about 4500 million years old. Nobody has ever dug to the centre (the deepest hole ever drilled is only about 12 km!), but by studying earthquake waves scientists have worked out what is inside.

The jigsaw puzzle continents

People once thought the Earth was only a few thousand years old and had never changed. Then in 1912 a German scientist called Alfred Wegener had a bold idea: millions of years ago all the land was joined in one giant supercontinent (now called Pangaea), which slowly broke apart. The pieces drifted to where they are today. This idea is called continental drift.

Evidence 1: shapes

The coastlines fit together like jigsaw pieces. Look at South America and Africa!

Evidence 2: rocks

The same types of rock appear on different continents exactly where they would join.

Evidence 3: fossils

Fossils of the same animals and plants are found on continents now separated by oceans.

Many scientists rejected Wegener's idea, because he could not explain what made the continents move. Good science needs a mechanism, not just evidence that something happened.

The answer: tectonic plates

We now know the crust is broken into huge pieces called tectonic plates. Oceanic plates lie under the oceans; continental plates carry the continents. The plates float on the hot, slowly flowing rock below. Heat from deep inside the Earth makes this rock churn very slowly, and that carries the plates along. (Below the surface, melted rock is called magma.)

Speed check

Plates move about 4 cm per year, about the speed your fingernails grow. That's tiny, but over 100 million years it adds up to 4000 km!

Drifting plates model

Float a few pieces of polystyrene on water in a heat-proof dish. Once they stop moving, heat the water gently from below and watch the pieces drift apart.

Polystyrene = plates, water = mantle, heat source = Earth's hot core. Weakness of the model: the real mantle is rock, not water, and real plates move far, far slower. Safety: an adult should handle the heating, wear safety glasses, and let the dish cool before touching it.

From idea to theory

For about 50 years, Wegener's idea stayed unproven. Then in the 1960s scientists mapping the ocean floor found long underwater mountain ridges where new rock was forming and spreading outwards. This led to the theory of plate tectonics: the crust is made of moving plates, pushed along by the slow flow of hot rock in the mantle. The theory finally explained how continental drift happens. It shows how science works: a good idea plus new evidence can change what everyone believes.

Think like a scientist: can you trust that website?

When you research online, ask two questions about each source:

  • Relevance: does it actually answer my question?
  • Reliability and bias: who wrote it? Is it backed by evidence most scientists accept, or is it one person's unusual opinion, or someone trying to sell something?

For example, a museum or university website saying "plates move because of flow in the mantle" matches the accepted evidence. A blog claiming "the continents only seem to move because the Earth is getting bigger" goes against a huge amount of evidence, so treat it with caution.

Understand it deeper

A boiled egg model. Crack the shell of a hard-boiled egg and cut it in half. The cracked shell is like the crust broken into plates, the white is like the mantle, and the yolk is like the core. Even the egg is too thick-shelled though: if the Earth were an apple, the crust would be thinner than the apple's skin.

How do we know what's inside? Nobody has been there, so scientists use clever indirect evidence:

Earthquake waves

Earthquakes send vibrations (seismic waves) right through the Earth. Instruments around the world record when they arrive. The waves speed up, slow down and bend as they pass through different layers. One type of wave cannot travel through liquids, and it never gets through the outer core. That's the evidence that the outer core is liquid!

Meteorites

Some meteorites are lumps of iron and nickel, left over from when the planets formed. This supports the idea that Earth's core is made of the same metals.

Volcanoes

Lava brings up material from deep below, giving clues about the mantle.

What actually moves the plates? The core heats the bottom of the mantle. Hot rock is less dense, so it slowly rises; near the top it cools, becomes denser and sinks again. This circular flow is called a convection current, just like soup moving in a pan on a stove. The plates on top get carried along, like toys floating on that soup. This is exactly what the polystyrene experiment models.

The outer core: Earth's giant magnet

The outer core is liquid iron and nickel, and it is constantly swirling as heat escapes from the centre. Moving liquid metal creates electric currents, and these produce Earth's magnetic field. (Exam tip: it's the outer core, not the inner core. The inner core is solid and can't flow.)

  • It makes compass needles point north, which has helped people navigate for centuries.
  • It acts like an invisible shield, deflecting harmful charged particles streaming from the Sun.
  • When some of those particles get through near the poles, they make the glowing auroras (northern and southern lights).

Myth-busters

The mantle is an ocean of runny lava.
Most of the mantle is solid-ish rock so hot it can flow very slowly, like extremely stiff putty, only a few cm a year. Only small pockets melt into magma.
Tectonic plates are the same as continents.
A plate can carry a continent, an ocean, or both. The plate boundaries often don't follow coastlines at all.
Wegener was wrong because people rejected his idea.
Being rejected doesn't make an idea wrong. Wegener's evidence was good; he was missing the mechanism. New evidence later proved the main idea right.

Where you meet it in real life

GPS measurementsSatellites now measure plate movement directly, millimetre by millimetre, every year.
Hazard mapsKnowing where plate boundaries are tells governments where to build stronger buildings and plan evacuations.
Finding resourcesMany metals, gems and oil deposits form near old or active plate boundaries.
IcelandIceland sits right on a boundary where two plates move apart. You can stand in the rift valley between them!

The traveller fossil

Fossils of tropical ferns have been found in Antarctica, which is frozen today. Suggest an explanation. (Antarctica was once much closer to the equator, and has drifted south over millions of years on its tectonic plate.)

Worked reasoning: focus on the process

Evaluate evidence · fossilsFossils of Mesosaurus, a small reptile that lived only in fresh water, are found in South America and in Africa, but nowhere else. Explain why this is strong evidence for continental drift.
  1. Pick out the important clueThe reptile lived only in fresh water, and it was small.
  2. Ask "what are the possible explanations?"(a) It swam across the ocean. (b) It evolved separately in two places. (c) The two continents were once joined.
  3. Test each one against the evidence(a) A small freshwater animal couldn't cross thousands of km of salty ocean. ✗ (b) Identical species evolving separately in two places is extremely unlikely. ✗ (c) If the land was joined, the animal simply lived in one area that later split. ✓
  4. Make your claim and link itOnly explanation (c) fits all the evidence.

A small freshwater reptile could not have crossed a salty ocean, so South America and Africa must have been joined when it lived, and have drifted apart since.

Ruling out other explanations makes your argument much stronger. This is how real scientists reason.

  3. Convert units step by step÷ 100 to get metres: 2 500 000 m. Then ÷ 1000 to get kilometres: 2500 km
  4. Sense-checkThe Atlantic is several thousand km wide today, and it began opening roughly 180 million years ago. 2500 km in 100 million years is the right size. ✓

About 2500 km. A tiny yearly movement becomes an ocean over geological time.

Big lesson: slow processes + very long time = huge changes.

Explain · being a scepticWhy was it reasonable for scientists in 1912 to doubt Wegener, even though he turned out to be right?
  1. Think about what makes a theory convincingEvidence that something happened, and an explanation of how it happens.
  2. Check what Wegener hadEvidence: yes (shapes, rocks, fossils). Mechanism: no. He couldn't say what force could push whole continents.
  3. Judge the scientists' responseAsking "how?" is good science. Extraordinary claims need strong support.
  4. What changed?In the 1960s, sea-floor mapping gave new evidence and a mechanism (mantle convection), and scientists changed their minds.

His idea lacked a mechanism, so doubting it was sensible. Scientists accepted it once new evidence explained how plates move. Science changes its mind when the evidence demands it.

Practice What is meant by "continental drift"?

Station 6.4

Restless Earth: mountains, volcanoes and quakes

  • How fold mountains and volcanoes form
  • Why earthquakes happen

The edges where two tectonic plates meet are called plate boundaries. Most geological change (changes to the rocks and land) happens there. Some changes are very slow, taking millions of years. Others are sudden and violent.

The edges of the Pacific Ocean are packed with volcanoes and earthquakes. This loop is nicknamed the Ring of Fire. Countries such as Japan, Indonesia, the Philippines and Chile all sit on it.

Three ways plates meet

Moving together

One plate may slide underneath the other. This is subduction. The sinking rock melts and becomes part of the mantle. Or the rocks crumple upwards into mountains.

Moving apart

The crust stretches, cracks and thins. Liquid rock from the mantle squeezes up through the gap as lava, cools and hardens into new crust. This builds a volcano.

Sliding past

The plates are huge and rough, so friction makes them stick. Force builds up for years, then they suddenly jerk free. That sudden movement is an earthquake.

Fold mountains

When two plates push into each other, the rock layers between them crumple and fold upwards, like pushing a tablecloth together on a table. The result is fold mountains, which can form on land or under the sea.

Mountain rangeWhereAge
HimalayasAsia10–25 million yrs
Rocky MountainsNorth America10–25 million yrs
Ural MountainsRussia> 200 million yrs

Young fold mountains are tall and jagged. Old ones like the Urals have been worn down by weather for so long that they are lower and rounder.

Inside a volcano

Magma or lava?

Same stuff, different place. Magma = liquid rock underground. Lava = liquid rock that has reached the surface.

If the magma is very thick and full of dissolved gas, pressure builds up until the volcano explodes violently, blasting out gas, ash and rock. Huge eruptions can even set off avalanches, earthquakes, and tsunamis if the volcano is near the sea. In 2018, part of Anak Krakatau in Indonesia collapsed into the sea and caused a tsunami.

Active

Has erupted recently and could erupt at any time.

Dormant / inactive

"Sleeping": hasn't erupted for a very long time, but might again.

Extinct

Will not erupt ever again.

Earthquakes

Some earthquakes are so slight that only scientific instruments notice them; others crack roads and flatten buildings. The strength of an earthquake is called its magnitude. It depends on the size of the faults (cracks) at the plate boundary and on how far the rocks move. In the biggest earthquakes, rocks can lurch tens of metres in seconds.

Model the boundaries

  • Cloth: push your hands together on a flat cloth and watch it fold into "mountains".
  • Paper: push two sheets together; one slides over the other, like subduction.
  • Modelling clay: push two flat slabs together and they crumple and thicken.
  • Chocolate bars with soft centres: push them together and the soft "mantle" oozes out. (Don't eat it if it's used in a lab!)

For each model, ask: what does it show well, and what does it get wrong?

The magnitude scale: ×10 each step

Earthquake magnitude is measured on a "times 10" scale. Each time the number goes up by 1, the earthquake is 10 times stronger. So the numbers grow much faster than they look!

Earthquakes under the sea

Many earthquakes happen under the oceans, at plate boundaries on the sea floor. When the sea floor suddenly jolts, it shoves a huge amount of water. This makes a tsunami: a series of giant waves that can race across the ocean and cause damage on coasts hundreds of kilometres away.

Understand it deeper

Why earthquakes are sudden. Bend a plastic ruler slowly. Energy is being stored in it. Nothing seems to happen… until it suddenly snaps or springs back and releases all that energy at once. Plates do the same: friction holds them stuck for years or centuries while energy builds up, then the rock gives way in seconds. The point underground where the rock breaks is the focus; the point on the surface directly above it is the epicentre, where shaking is usually strongest.

Why do plates melt when they sink? In subduction, the plate is pushed deep down where it's much hotter. Water carried down with the plate also helps the rock melt more easily. This new magma rises, which is why there's often a line of volcanoes next to a subduction zone, like the Andes in South America and the volcanoes of Indonesia.

Myth-busters

Scientists can predict exactly when an earthquake will strike.
They can say where earthquakes are likely and roughly how often, but not the exact day or hour.
A magnitude 8 earthquake is twice as strong as a magnitude 4.
It's 4 steps up the ×10 scale: 10 × 10 × 10 × 10 = 10 000 times stronger.
Volcanoes only happen on land.
Most volcanic activity is under the sea, at boundaries where plates move apart along the ocean floor.
Dormant means safe.
Dormant only means "sleeping". Some volcanoes thought dormant for centuries have erupted with very little warning.

Where you meet it in real life

Earthquake-proof buildingsFlexible steel frames, cross-bracing and rubber "base isolators" let buildings sway instead of snapping, like a tree bending in the wind.
Tsunami warning buoysSensors on the sea floor detect sudden pressure changes and send alerts so coasts can evacuate.
Volcano watchingScientists measure gas, ground swelling and tiny tremors to spot an eruption coming.
Rich farmlandVolcanic ash breaks down into very fertile soil, which is why many people farm near volcanoes in Indonesia and Italy.
Geothermal energyIceland uses heat from underground near its plate boundary to generate electricity and heat homes.
Close to homeSingapore isn't on a plate boundary, but tall buildings sometimes sway gently from big earthquakes in Sumatra, hundreds of km away.

Young and old mountains

The Himalayas are still getting a few millimetres taller each year, but the Ural Mountains are not. Suggest why. (The Indian plate is still pushing into the Eurasian plate. The plates that made the Urals stopped colliding long ago, so weathering slowly wears them down.)

Worked reasoning: focus on the process

Method · the ×10 scaleHow many times stronger is a magnitude 9 earthquake than a magnitude 5?
  1. Find the number of steps9 − 5 = 4 steps
  2. Each step multiplies by 10So multiply 10 by itself 4 times: 10 × 10 × 10 × 10
  3. Solve= 10 000
  4. ShortcutThe answer is 1 followed by as many zeros as there are steps. 4 steps → 10 000.
  5. Common error checkDid you add (5 + 4 = 9 → "4 times")? That's wrong. On this scale you multiply, you don't add.

A magnitude 9 earthquake is 10 000 times stronger than a magnitude 5.

Explain · map reasoningJapan has many earthquakes and volcanoes. Australia has very few. Use your knowledge of tectonic plates to explain why.
  1. Recall the ruleEarthquakes and volcanoes mostly happen at plate boundaries.
  2. Apply it to JapanJapan sits where several plates meet (it's on the Ring of Fire). Plates are pushing and sliding under each other right below it.
  3. Apply it to AustraliaAustralia is in the middle of the Indo-Australian plate, far from its edges.
  4. Compare and concludeClose to boundaries means lots of plate movement, so lots of activity. Far from boundaries means little activity.

Japan lies on boundaries between several plates, where the plates move against each other. Australia lies near the centre of one plate, so it experiences far fewer earthquakes and volcanoes.

Evaluate · living near a volcanoMillions of people live near active volcanoes. Evaluate whether this is a sensible choice.
  1. "Evaluate" means weigh up both sidesMake two lists, then reach a judgement.
  2. BenefitsVery fertile soil for farming; tourism jobs; geothermal energy; families may have lived there for generations.
  3. RisksEruptions, lava flows, ash clouds, poisonous gases, tsunamis near the coast.

  5. JudgeGive a conclusion that depends on the facts ("it can be sensible if…").

It can be a sensible choice because of the fertile soil and jobs, provided the volcano is closely monitored and there are good warning and evacuation plans. Without these, the risk to life is high.

There is no single right answer to "evaluate" questions. Marks come from showing both sides and justifying your judgement.

Station 6.5

Eclipses: when the lights go out

  • How solar eclipses happen
  • How lunar eclipses happen

Shadows first

Light travels in straight lines, called rays. An opaque object doesn't let light pass through it. When an opaque object blocks the light from a source, a dark area forms behind it: a shadow.

Watch the shadow of an aeroplane racing over a field. The ground in the shadow goes dark because the metal plane blocks the Sun's rays, while the grass around it stays bright. Stand in its path and it would suddenly go dim, then bright again.

Remember: the Sun gives out its own light. The Moon does not. We only see the Moon because it reflects sunlight.

Solar eclipse: the Moon hides the Sun

A solar eclipse happens when the Moon comes between the Sun and the Earth. The Moon is opaque rock, so it blocks the sunlight and casts its shadow onto the Earth.

Total solar eclipse

Seen from the very middle of the Moon's shadow. All direct sunlight is blocked, the sky goes dark in daytime, and only the Sun's glowing outer edge (the corona) shows.

Partial solar eclipse

Seen from further out, where only some of the Sun's rays are blocked. The Sun looks like a cookie with a bite taken out.

Never look directly at the Sun

Not even during an eclipse. The light is strong enough to permanently damage your eyes. Use certified eclipse glasses or a pinhole projector.

Lunar eclipse: Earth's shadow covers the Moon

A lunar eclipse happens when the Earth comes between the Sun and the Moon. The Earth blocks the sunlight, and its shadow falls on the Moon. During a total lunar eclipse the Moon often turns a coppery red.

Solar eclipseLunar eclipse
What's in the middle?MoonEarth
Whose shadow?Moon's shadow on EarthEarth's shadow on Moon
When can you see it?Daytime only (the Sun must be up)Night-time (the Moon must be up)
Safe to look at?No! Eye protection neededYes

Why not every month?

The Moon orbits the Earth about every 27 days, so you might expect an eclipse every month. But the Moon's orbit is tilted slightly compared to Earth's path around the Sun. Most months the Moon passes a little above or below the line. Eclipses only happen when the Sun, Earth and Moon line up exactly in a straight line.

Ancient eclipse predictors

About 3000 years ago, astronomers in the Middle East noticed that eclipse patterns repeat every 223 lunar months. With a lunar month of 29.5 days, that's 223 × 29.5 ≈ 6579 days, about 18 years. Spotting patterns in observations is how scientists make predictions! Working backwards, modern scientists have even used a recorded eclipse to date an ancient battle in Greece to 28 May 585 BCE.

Eclipse in your bedroom

Use a lamp as the Sun, a football as the Earth and a tennis ball on a string as the Moon. Darken the room.

  • Swing the tennis ball between the lamp and the football: its shadow lands on the football. That's a solar eclipse.
  • Move the tennis ball behind the football, into its shadow. That's a lunar eclipse.

Safety: the lamp bulb gets hot, and never use the real Sun for this.

Drawing an eclipse diagram: checklist

Exam questions often ask you to draw an eclipse. Include:

  • Sun, Earth and Moon in the right order, in a straight line, and labelled
  • Sun drawn biggest and Moon smallest
  • Straight rays of light from the Sun, drawn with a ruler
  • The shadow shaded in
  • For a solar eclipse, labels showing where a total eclipse and a partial eclipse are seen

Understand it deeper

An amazing coincidence. The Sun is about 400 times wider than the Moon, but it is also about 400 times further away. So from Earth, they look almost exactly the same size in the sky! That's why the Moon can cover the Sun perfectly in a total eclipse. Try it: close one eye and hold up your thumb. Your small thumb can hide a huge building far away.

Why does the Moon turn red? During a total lunar eclipse, a little sunlight still gets to the Moon by bending through Earth's atmosphere. The atmosphere scatters away blue light and lets red light through, the same reason sunsets are red. In a way, the Moon is lit by all the sunrises and sunsets on Earth at once!

Umbra, penumbra and antumbra (exam favourite!)

Because the Sun is a big, wide light source (not a tiny dot), the Moon's shadow has different parts. Exams love these words:

Part of shadowWhat it isWhat you'd see from there
UmbraThe darkest, central part. All of the Sun's light is blocked.Total solar eclipse
PenumbraThe lighter outer part. Only some of the Sun's light is blocked.Partial solar eclipse
AntumbraThe region beyond the tip of the umbra, when the Moon is a bit further from Earth and looks slightly smaller than the Sun.Annular eclipse: a bright "ring of fire" around the Moon

Memory trick: Umbra = um… totally dark. Penumbra = "almost" (like a peninsula, which is almost an island). Antumbra = "beyond" the umbra.

Solar eclipse: seen by few

The Moon's shadow on Earth is small, at most a few hundred km wide for the total part. Only people in that narrow path see a total eclipse, and it lasts just a few minutes.

Lunar eclipse: seen by many

Earth's shadow is big enough to cover the whole Moon. Anyone on the night side of Earth can see it, and it can last over an hour.

Myth-busters

The Moon's phases (crescent, half, gibbous) are caused by Earth's shadow.
Phases are caused by how much of the Moon's sunlit half we can see as it orbits. Earth's shadow only reaches the Moon during a lunar eclipse.
The Moon makes its own light.
The Moon only reflects sunlight. That's why it goes dark when Earth blocks the sunlight.
It's safe to look at a solar eclipse through sunglasses.
Sunglasses are nowhere near dark enough. Use certified eclipse glasses or a pinhole projector only.

Where you meet it in real life

Testing EinsteinIn 1919, astronomers photographed stars near the Sun during a total eclipse and showed that the Sun's gravity bends starlight, as Einstein had predicted.
Studying the coronaThe Sun's outer atmosphere is normally hidden by its glare. Eclipses let scientists study it.
Dating historyRecords of ancient eclipses help historians fix exact dates of events, like the battle in Greece in 585 BCE.
Pinhole projectorA tiny hole in card projects a safe image of the Sun onto paper, because light travels in straight lines.

Moon-phase detective

A solar eclipse can only happen at new moon, and a lunar eclipse only at full moon. Can you work out why from the order of the Sun, Earth and Moon? (Solar: the Moon is between us and the Sun, so its lit side faces away from us: new moon. Lunar: the Moon is behind Earth, opposite the Sun, so its whole lit side faces us: full moon.)

Worked reasoning: focus on the process

Reason from a diagramDuring a solar eclipse, what phase is the Moon in? Explain how you know.
  1. Draw itSketch the order for a solar eclipse: Sun · Moon · Earth, in a line.
  2. Shade the lit halfThe Moon's sunlit half always faces the Sun, so shade the side of the Moon facing the Sun.
  3. Look from EarthWhich side of the Moon faces Earth? The dark side, away from the Sun.
  4. Name itWhen we see none of the lit half, that's a new moon.

The Moon is a new moon, because its sunlit side faces the Sun and away from Earth.

A quick sketch turns a hard "imagine it" question into an easy "look at it" question.

Explain · comparingIn your whole life you might see many lunar eclipses from home, but perhaps no total solar eclipse at all. Explain why.
  1. Compare the shadowsSolar eclipse: the Moon's shadow on Earth. The Moon is small, so the dark centre of its shadow is only a narrow strip on Earth.
  2. Who can see each one?Total solar: only people inside that narrow strip. Lunar: everyone on Earth's night side, which is about half the planet.
  3. Link to "from home"The narrow strip of a solar eclipse lands somewhere different each time, so it rarely crosses the same town.
  4. Conclude with a comparison wordUse "whereas" or "but" to show you're comparing.

A lunar eclipse can be seen from the whole night side of Earth, whereas a total solar eclipse is only seen along a narrow path covered by the Moon's small shadow. That path rarely passes over any one place.

Calculate · ancient predictionsAncient astronomers knew eclipse patterns repeat every 223 lunar months. A lunar month is 29.5 days. About how many years is that?
  1. Months to days223 × 29.5. Split it to make it easier: 223 × 30 = 6690, then subtract 223 × 0.5 = 111.5. So 6690 − 111.5 = 6578.5 days.
  2. Days to years6578.5 ÷ 365 ≈ 18 years
  3. Sense-checkA year has about 12.4 lunar months, and 223 ÷ 12.4 ≈ 18, so about 18 years is right. ✓
  4. Think about the scienceWhy does a pattern repeat? Because the Sun, Earth and Moon return to almost the same arrangement. Spotting a repeating pattern lets you predict the future.

About 6580 days ≈ 18 years. Ancient astronomers used this pattern to predict eclipses without telescopes.
