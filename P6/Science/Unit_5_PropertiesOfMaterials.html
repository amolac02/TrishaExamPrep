Station 5.1

Metals and non-metals: two families of materials

  • Properties of metals
  • Properties of non-metals
  • Matching properties to uses

Look around the room. Door handles, coins, scissors, a laptop case: all metals. Air, the graphite in your pencil, the oxygen you breathe: non-metals. Materials are the substances that objects are made from. Scientists sort the elements into two big groups, metals and non-metals, by their properties (what they are like and how they behave).

What makes a metal a metal?

Shiny

Freshly cut or polished metals are shiny. Old metal can look dull because its surface reacts with the air.

Strong and tough

Metals don't shatter (break into pieces) when dropped. They can hold heavy loads, which is why bridges use iron and steel.

Malleable

Malleable means it can be hammered or bent into shape without breaking. A blacksmith shapes hot iron this way.

Ductile

Ductile means it can be pulled out into long, thin wires. Copper is very ductile.

Sonorous

Sonorous means it makes a ringing sound when hit, like a bell or cymbals.

High melting point

Most metals need a lot of heat to melt. Mercury is the odd one out: it's a metal that is liquid at room temperature.

Good conductors

Metals conduct (let through) heat and electricity well. That's why a metal railing feels cold: it carries heat away from your hand quickly.

Some are magnetic

Only iron, nickel and cobalt (and steel, which contains iron) are magnetic. Most metals, like copper and aluminium, are not.

What are non-metals like?

Non-metals are much more varied. Many are gases (oxygen, nitrogen, helium, chlorine). The solid ones usually share these properties:

Dull

They don't reflect light well, so they look dull, like a lump of sulfur or charcoal.

Brittle

Brittle means it breaks or shatters easily when hit or bent. Hit a lump of sulfur with a hammer and it crumbles.

Insulators

Most do not conduct heat or electricity. They are insulators, which makes them useful for pan handles and wire coatings.

Low melting points

Solid non-metals usually melt and boil at much lower temperatures than metals.

Where are they on the Periodic Table? Metals are on the left and in the middle; non-metals are on the right.

The exceptions

Science loves exceptions. Graphite (a form of carbon, a non-metal) conducts electricity, which is why pencil "lead" can complete a circuit. Diamond (also carbon) is the hardest natural material, not brittle at all. Mercury is a liquid metal. So always look at several properties together before deciding.

Property detective

Collect metal objects at home: a spoon, scissors, a key, foil, a paper clip. For each one, write: what it is used for, what metal it's probably made of, and which property makes it right for the job. Then find two things with plastic handles and explain why the handle isn't metal.

Understand it deeper

Why do metals feel cold? A metal railing and a wooden bench in the same shade are at the same temperature. The metal feels colder because it is a good conductor: it carries heat away from your hand much faster. Your skin senses heat leaving, not the actual temperature.

"Dull" doesn't always mean non-metal. Old metals get a dull coating because the surface reacts with oxygen in the air (like a tarnished spoon). Scratch or polish it and the shine comes back. A non-metal stays dull all the way through.

Myth-busters

All metals are magnetic.
Only iron, nickel, cobalt (and steel) are. A magnet won't pick up copper, gold or aluminium cans.
All metals are solid.
Mercury is a liquid metal at room temperature.
If it makes a ringing sound, it must be metal.
Glass bottles and ceramic cups ring too. Use several tests before deciding.
Metal feels cold because metal is colder.
It's the same temperature. It conducts heat away from your hand faster.

The best test: does it conduct electricity?

Of all the tests, the circuit test is the most reliable for solids, because almost all metals conduct and almost all solid non-metals don't.

Materials investigation

For each material ask: Shiny or dull? Rings when tapped? Bends or snaps? Feels cold or warm? Lights the lamp? Record the answers in a table with a final column: metal or non-metal? Decide using all the evidence, not one test.

Writing a good comparison

A comparison puts the two things side by side in the same sentence. Useful phrases: whereas, but, however, higher than, compared with.

Not a comparison

"Metals are shiny. Non-metals are dull."

A comparison

"Metals are shiny, whereas non-metals are dull. Metals also have higher melting points than most non-metals."

Understand it deeper

Using data like a scientist. Sometimes you can't touch the substance, but you're given information: melting point, boiling point, appearance, conductivity. Build your decision from several clues, then name the substance only if the clues point clearly to one.

Worked reasoning: focus on the process

Identify · from dataSubstance X is shiny, conducts electricity and melts at −39 °C. Substance Y is black, brittle, but also conducts electricity. Are X and Y metals?
  1. Look at XShiny ✓ metal. Conducts ✓ metal. Melts at −39 °C means it's a liquid at room temperature. Unusual, but there is one liquid metal: mercury.
  2. Look at YBlack and brittle → non-metal clues. Conducts → metal clue. Mixed evidence!
  3. Weigh the evidenceTwo non-metal clues against one. And we know a non-metal that conducts: graphite (carbon).

X is a metal (mercury). Y is a non-metal (graphite), the exception that conducts electricity.

When clues disagree, count them and think of the known exceptions.

Evaluate · test planWhich single test is most useful for telling metals and non-metals apart, and why?
  1. List the testsLook (shine), tap (ring), bend, feel (cold), conduct electricity.
  2. Find each test's weak spotShine: old metals look dull. Ring: glass rings too. Bend: thick metal won't bend by hand. Feel: hard to judge.
  3. Choose the most reliableThe circuit test gives a clear yes/no and has only one common exception (graphite).

The electrical conductivity test, because nearly all metals conduct and nearly all solid non-metals don't, and the result is clear.

Station 5.3

Alloys: metals mixed to be better

  • What an alloy is
  • Why alloys are harder (particle theory)
  • Alloys in everyday life

Pure gold is so soft you could scratch it with a fingernail. So how can a gold ring survive years of wear? The answer is an alloy: a mixture of a metal with other elements (usually other metals), made by melting them together. The atoms mix but don't bond together, so an alloy is a mixture, not a compound.

Quick recap: element, compound, mixture

Element: only one kind of atom (copper). Compound: different atoms chemically joined (water, H₂O). Mixture: different substances together but not chemically joined (sea water, brass).

Why are alloys harder? Particle theory

This one idea explains a lot. Layers that slide easily make a pure metal malleable and ductile, but also soft. Jam the layers and the alloy becomes harder and stronger, though usually a little less malleable.

Famous alloys

AlloyMade fromWhy it's betterUses
BronzeCopper + tinHarder than copper or tin; resists corrosionStatues, bells, ship parts, medals

Steel is an unusual alloy because carbon is a non-metal. Even a tiny amount of carbon makes iron much harder.

Gold and carats

The purity of gold is measured in carats, out of 24. 24 carat gold is pure gold. 18 carat gold has 18 parts gold out of 24, and 6 parts other metals like copper or silver.

18 carat: 18 ÷ 24 = 0.75 → 75% gold
9 carat: 9 ÷ 24 = 0.375 → 37.5% gold

Lower carat gold is harder (better for rings that get knocked about), but contains less gold, so it's cheaper.

Mixtures melt over a range

A pure substance melts at one fixed temperature: copper melts at 1085 °C and zinc at about 420 °C. Brass is a mixture, and the amounts of copper and zinc can vary, so brass melts over a range of temperatures (roughly 900–1000 °C). The same is true for bronze.

Coins and medals

"Silver" coins usually contain no silver at all! They're often made of copper and nickel alloys, which are much harder (so they don't wear away in your pocket) and much cheaper.

Bubble raft model

Put a little washing-up liquid water in a shallow dish. Use a straw or syringe to blow lots of equal-sized small bubbles until they pack into neat rows: these are metal atoms. Push the rows gently with a pencil and watch them slide. Now add one much bigger bubble in the middle and look at how it disrupts the pattern. That's your alloy!

Limitation of the model: bubbles pop and drift, and real atoms are packed in three dimensions, not a flat layer.

Understand it deeper

A traffic jam analogy. Picture cars of the same size driving in neat lanes: they can glide past each other. Now park a big lorry across two lanes. Everything gets stuck. The lorry is the different-sized atom in an alloy. (Where the analogy breaks down: atoms don't drive forward on their own; the layers only slide when a force pushes them.)

Trade-offs. Making an alloy is a design choice. You often gain hardness and strength but lose a little malleability or conductivity. Engineers choose the alloy that fits the job best.

Myth-busters

An alloy is a compound.
An alloy is a mixture. The atoms mix but don't chemically bond, and the amounts can vary.
Pure metals are always stronger.
Pure metals are often softer because their layers slide easily. Alloys are usually harder.
24 carat means the strongest gold.
24 carat is the purest gold, but also the softest.

Worked reasoning: focus on the process

Explain · particle theoryUse particle theory to explain why steel is harder than pure iron.
  1. Describe the pure metalIn pure iron, all the atoms are the same size and arranged in regular layers.
  2. Say what a force doesWhen a force is applied, the layers can slide over each other easily, so pure iron is quite soft.
  3. Describe the alloyIn steel, carbon atoms are a different size and disrupt the regular layers.
  4. Link to the propertyThe layers can't slide easily, so steel is harder.

In pure iron, the same-sized atoms form layers that slide easily. In steel, different-sized carbon atoms disrupt the layers, so they can't slide easily, making steel harder.

Exam answers need all four links: same-sized atoms → layers slide → different-sized atoms disrupt → layers can't slide.

Calculate · caratsA necklace is 22 carat gold and has a mass of 12 g. About how many grams of pure gold does it contain?
  1. Turn carats into a fraction22 carat = 22 parts out of 24 → 22/24.
  2. Simplify22/24 = 11/12.
  3. Multiply by the mass11/12 × 12 g = 11 g.
  4. Sense-check22 carat is nearly pure, so the gold should be nearly all of the 12 g. 11 g ✓

About 11 g of pure gold (and 1 g of other metals).

Evaluate · choosing a materialWhy are aeroplanes made from aluminium alloys and not pure aluminium or steel?
  1. What does a plane need?Light (to fly using less fuel) and strong (to cope with huge forces).
  2. Test each optionPure aluminium: light ✓ but too soft ✗. Steel: strong ✓ but heavy ✗.
  3. Find the best fitAn aluminium alloy (duralumin): light ✓ and strong ✓.

Aluminium alloys are both light and strong. Pure aluminium is too soft, and steel is too heavy.

Station 5.4

Separating mixtures: using properties to pull things apart

  • Choose the right method
  • Choose the right apparatus
  • Work safely

A mixture contains different substances that are not chemically joined. That's good news: it means we can separate them, using a difference in their properties. Iron is magnetic but sulfur isn't, so a magnet separates them. Sand doesn't dissolve but salt does, so water and a filter separate them.

Filtration

Filtration separates an insoluble solid (one that doesn't dissolve) from a liquid. A filter paper sits in a filter funnel over a conical flask. The tiny holes in the paper let water and dissolved particles through, but trap bigger solid grains.

Residue

The solid left in the filter paper (for example, sand).

Filtrate

The clear liquid that passes through (for example, salty water).

Evaporation

Evaporation gets a dissolved solid back out of a solution. Heat the solution in an evaporating basin so the water turns to vapour and escapes, leaving the solid behind. Copper sulfate solution left in a warm place slowly leaves blue crystals.

Tip: heat gently until about half the water has gone, then leave it to finish in a warm place. Boiling it dry can make hot crystals spit out.

Distillation

Distillation gets the pure liquid back from a solution. It works because the substances have different boiling points.

The two changes of state

Evaporating/boiling: liquid → gas (in the heated flask). Condensing: gas → liquid (in the condenser). Distillation is just these two changes, one after the other.

Separating a three-part mixture: sand, salt and water

  1. Filter the mixture. Sand stays in the filter paper (residue). Salty water passes through (filtrate).
  2. Leave the filter paper somewhere warm to dry the sand.
  3. Evaporate the filtrate in an evaporating basin to get the salt.
  4. Want the water too? Distil the filtrate instead of evaporating it.

Safety when separating

  • Wear safety glasses when heating.
  • Stand up when heating so you can move away quickly.
  • Use tongs on hot evaporating basins.
  • Don't heat until dry: hot crystals can spit.
  • Sweep broken glass with a dustpan and brush, never with bare hands.

Understand it deeper

Why does filtering work? When salt dissolves, it breaks into particles far too small to see, and they spread through the water. They're small enough to pass through the holes in filter paper. Sand grains are millions of times bigger, so they get stuck. Filtering separates by particle size.

Why does distillation work? It separates by boiling point. Water boils at 100 °C; salt would need over 1400 °C. Only the water turns to gas and leaves the flask.

Myth-busters

Filtering salty water removes the salt.
Dissolved salt passes through filter paper. You need evaporation or distillation.
When salt dissolves, it disappears.
The salt is still there as tiny particles. Taste it, or evaporate the water to get it back.
Evaporation and distillation are the same.
Evaporation keeps the solid and loses the water. Distillation keeps both by collecting the water too.

  2. What do we want to come out?Liquid water.

Station 5.5

Acids and alkalis: chemical opposites

  • Properties of acids and alkalis
  • Hazard symbols
  • Working safely

Bite into a lemon and your face screws up. That sour taste comes from an acid. Acids are everywhere: citrus fruits contain citric acid, vinegar contains ethanoic acid, and fizzy drinks contain acids too. These are weak acids, safe to eat in small amounts. Never taste anything in a lab, though!

Acids

Taste sour (food acids only).
Lab acids: hydrochloric, sulfuric, nitric acid.
Strong acids are corrosive: they destroy living tissue such as skin and cause chemical burns.

Alkalis

Feel soapy (never test this on purpose!).
Found in many cleaning products: soap, washing powder, oven cleaner.
Lab alkalis: sodium hydroxide, potassium hydroxide, calcium hydroxide.
Strong alkalis are corrosive too.

Acids and alkalis are chemical opposites. When they are mixed in the right amounts, they cancel each other out. Whether a substance is acidic or alkaline is one of its chemical properties.

Dilute means less dangerous, not safe

Adding water to an acid or alkali makes it dilute, which makes it less dangerous. But dilute acids can still be harmful (cause harm if they get into the body) or irritant (make skin and eyes sore and red). If you spill some, wash it off with lots of water. The water dilutes it and carries it away.

Hazard warning symbols

Dangerous chemicals carry a red diamond warning symbol. These symbols are used worldwide, so you can understand the danger whatever language you speak.

Explosive

May explode if heated or near a flame.

Flammable

Catches fire easily.

Oxidising

Helps other things burn; gives off heat with other substances.

Corrosive

Destroys living tissue (skin, eyes) and attacks metals.

Understand it deeper

Corrosive vs irritant. Think of it as a scale of damage. Corrosive = destroys tissue (a chemical burn). Irritant = makes skin red and sore, but doesn't destroy it. Harmful = can make you ill. A strong acid is corrosive; diluting it can turn it into an irritant.

Why have symbols at all? A picture works in any language, and you can recognise it from across a room. Lorries carrying acids also display warning signs, so emergency teams know immediately how to deal with a spill.

Myth-busters

Only acids are dangerous; alkalis are safe.
Strong alkalis like sodium hydroxide are corrosive too. Oven cleaner can burn skin.
All acids are dangerous.
Weak acids in lemons, vinegar and yoghurt are safe to eat. Strength and concentration matter.
If acid spills on your skin, wipe it off with a dry cloth.
Wash with lots of water. Wiping can spread it, and water dilutes it.

Where you meet it in real life

Station 5.6

Indicators and the pH scale: chemistry in colour

  • Tell acids from alkalis
  • Make and use indicators
  • Use the pH scale

Three beakers of clear liquid look exactly the same. One is water, one is acid, one is alkali. You can't taste them and you shouldn't touch them. How do you tell them apart? Add a few drops of an indicator: a substance that changes colour depending on whether it's in an acid or an alkali.

Litmus

Litmus is a dye that comes from tiny organisms called lichens. It's used as a solution or soaked into paper.

In acidIn neutral (water)In alkali
Litmus solutionRedPurpleBlue
Red litmus paperStays redStays redTurns blue
Blue litmus paperTurns redStays blueStays blue

Litmus paper trap (exam favourite!)

If red litmus paper stays red, the liquid could be acid OR neutral. You can't tell which from that test alone. You need blue litmus too: if blue turns red, it's an acid; if blue stays blue, it's neutral.

A neutral substance is neither acid nor alkali. Pure water and sugar solution are neutral.

Homemade indicators

Many brightly coloured plants contain natural indicators: red cabbage, blackcurrants, beetroot. Red cabbage juice turns red/pink in acids, stays purple-blue in neutral water and turns green to yellow in alkalis.

Make red cabbage indicator

  1. Chop some red cabbage and crush it in a mortar and pestle (or blend it at home with an adult).
  2. Add a little warm water (in school, ethanol may be used; it's flammable, so no flames nearby!).
  3. Keep crushing until the liquid is deep purple. Strain it.
  4. Add a few drops to lemon juice, water, and a little dissolved baking soda. Record the colours.

Limitation: a coloured liquid like cola, coffee or tea hides the colour change, so this indicator can't test those. Use a pH meter instead.

Universal indicator and the pH scale

Litmus only tells you if something is acid or alkali. Universal indicator is a mixture of several indicators, so it shows many colours and tells you how acidic or alkaline something is. We match the colour to a number on the pH scale, which runs from 0 to 14.

01234567891011121314
← stronger acidneutral 7stronger alkali →
pHColour of universal indicatorTypeExamples

12–14Purple / violetStrongly alkalineSodium hydroxide, oven cleaner

The exact colours vary a little between brands of universal indicator, so always use the chart that comes with it.

Remember the direction

Below 7 = acid, 7 = neutral, above 7 = alkali. The further from 7, the stronger it is. So pH 1 is a stronger acid than pH 5. Lower number, stronger acid. That feels backwards, so learn it carefully!

Understand it deeper

Why use more than one indicator? Each simple indicator changes colour at a different pH. By testing with two, you can narrow down the pH. For example, methyl orange is red below about pH 3 and yellow above about pH 4.5, and phenolphthalein is colourless below about pH 8 and pink above it. If a liquid turns methyl orange red and leaves phenolphthalein colourless, its pH must be about 3 or lower.

Neutralisation. When you add an alkali to an acid little by little, the pH rises towards 7. At pH 7 the acid has been cancelled out. This is called neutralisation, and it's how indigestion tablets work.

Myth-busters

A higher pH number means a stronger acid.
For acids, it's the opposite: lower pH = stronger acid.
If red litmus stays red, the liquid is an acid.
It could be acid or neutral. Test with blue litmus or universal indicator to be sure.
Litmus can tell you how strong an acid is.
Litmus only says acid or alkali. Universal indicator shows the strength (pH).
Water is a weak acid.
Pure water is neutral, pH 7.

Where you meet it in real life

Swimming poolsStaff test the pH every day and keep it close to neutral (about 7.2–7.8) so it doesn't sting your eyes.
GardeningHydrangea flowers turn blue in acidic soil and pink in alkaline soil: a natural indicator!
ShampooMany are made slightly acidic to be gentle on hair and skin.
AquariumsFish need the right pH; owners test the water with indicator kits.

The invisible ink

Write a message on paper with lemon juice and let it dry. Spray it with red cabbage juice. What will you see, and why? (The message appears pink/red, because the lemon juice is acidic and the indicator changes colour where the acid is.)

Identify · litmus testVinegar, sugar solution and detergent are tested with red litmus paper. Two show no change. Which two, and why?
  1. Classify each liquid firstVinegar = acid. Sugar solution = neutral. Detergent = alkali.
  2. Recall what red litmus doesIt turns blue only in an alkali. In acid or neutral liquids, it stays red.
  3. Apply itDetergent turns it blue. Vinegar and sugar solution leave it red.

Vinegar (an acid) and sugar solution (neutral). Red litmus only changes colour (to blue) in an alkali.

Always classify each substance first, then apply the indicator rule.

Use the scale · placing on pHMark likely pH values for vinegar, sugar solution and detergent, and say which turns universal indicator violet.
  1. ClassifyVinegar = weak acid; sugar solution = neutral; detergent = alkali.
  2. Pick a sensible number for eachWeak acid → between 3 and 6 (vinegar about 3). Neutral → exactly 7. Alkali → above 7 (detergent about 9–11).
  3. Match the colourViolet/purple is at the alkaline end, so it must be the detergent (a strongly alkaline one).

Vinegar about pH 3, sugar solution pH 7, detergent about pH 10. Violet = detergent, because violet means strongly alkaline.

Analyse · two indicatorsA liquid makes phenolphthalein stay colourless and turns methyl orange yellow. What can you say about its pH?
  1. Use the first cluePhenolphthalein colourless → pH below about 8.
  2. Use the second clueMethyl orange yellow → pH above about 4.5.
  3. Combine themThe pH must satisfy both: between about 4.5 and 8.
  4. Say what you still don't knowIt could be weakly acidic, neutral, or very weakly alkaline. Universal indicator would narrow it down.

The pH is between about 4.5 and 8. These two indicators can't say exactly; universal indicator would give a closer value.
